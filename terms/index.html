From the carousel · 6 terms

The six, plain English

What it is, an everyday example, the developer version, and the trap

Start here. These six show up in every AI conversation.

  • Modelthe thing that predicts

    A model is a math function that learned its rules from examples instead of someone writing them by hand. You give it an input (an email, a photo, a sentence) and it gives back a prediction (spam or not, cat or dog, the next word). ChatGPT, your spam filter and Netflix recommendations are all models.

    Everyday example: Your email's spam filter was never told the rule 'free money = spam'. It saw millions of emails labelled spam or not, and worked the patterns out itself.

    Dev: A function whose logic was learned, not written

    Watch out: It only knows the patterns in the data it was trained on. Ask about something it never saw and it will still confidently guess.

    In one line
    ŷ = f(x; θ)

  • Parametersthe knobs

    Parameters are the internal numbers inside a model that decide what it outputs. Training is just the process of setting them. A tiny model has a handful; large language models have billions. They're also called weights.

    Everyday example: Think of a mixing desk with billions of sliders. Each slider barely matters alone, but together their positions decide every answer the model gives.

    Dev: Config values the program tunes by itself

    Watch out: More parameters isn't automatically smarter. A smaller model trained on better data often beats a bigger one trained on worse data.

    What they are
    θ = [w1, w2, …, wn]

  • Traininglearning from mistakes

    Training is how a model learns. Show it an example, compare its guess to the right answer, measure how wrong it was (the loss), then nudge every parameter a tiny bit in the direction that makes it less wrong. Repeat that millions of times and the guesses get good.

    Everyday example: Like learning to throw darts: throw, see how far off you landed, adjust your aim a little, throw again. Thousands of throws later you're hitting the board.

    Dev: An optimisation loop over your dataset

    Watch out: Garbage in, garbage out. If the training data is biased, messy or wrong, the model learns those mistakes too.

    The loop
    for x, y in data: loss = (model(x) - y) ** 2 θ -= lr * grad(loss, θ)

  • Inferenceusing it

    Inference is running a trained model on new inputs to get an answer. Every time you send ChatGPT a message, that's inference. The parameters are frozen, so the model isn't learning anything from you in that moment.

    Everyday example: Training is studying for the exam. Inference is sitting the exam: you use what you learned, but you're not learning anything new while you write.

    Dev: Calling the function in production

    Watch out: Inference is far cheaper than training, but it isn't free. Every answer still costs compute, which is why AI products have usage limits.

    In code
    answer = model(new_input) # θ stays frozen

  • Embeddingsmeaning as numbers

    An embedding turns something (a word, a sentence, an image, a song, a user) into a list of numbers, so a computer can compare meaning with math. Things with similar meaning end up with similar numbers, close together in that space.

    Everyday example: On Spotify, songs you like sit near each other on a giant invisible map. 'Songs like this' just means 'the nearest points on the map'.

    Dev: A vector you compare with cosine similarity

    Watch out: Close means similar to the model, not necessarily true or correct. 'Doctor' and 'nurse' can sit close because they appear in similar sentences, biases included.

    Similarity
    sim(a, b) = a · b‖a‖ ‖b‖

  • Overfittingmemorising, not learning

    Overfitting is when a model memorises its training data, including the noise and flukes, instead of learning the general pattern. It looks brilliant on the examples it has seen and falls apart on anything new.

    Everyday example: A student who memorised last year's answer key aces the practice test, then fails the real exam the moment the questions change.

    Dev: Tests that only pass on your fixtures

    Watch out: Always check your model on data it never saw during training. A great training score on its own proves nothing.

    The tell
    train loss ↓   test loss ↑

How models learn · 8 terms

Under the hood

What actually happens during training

The words behind "we trained a model". Read these in order: each one builds on the last.

  • Neural networklayers of tiny calculators

    A model built from layers of simple units ("neurons"). Each one takes numbers in, weights them, and passes a result to the next layer. Stack enough layers and the network can learn very complex patterns, like recognising faces or writing text.

    Everyday example: A relay of people, each making one tiny judgement and passing it on. Nobody sees the whole picture, but together they recognise a cat.

    Dev: Matrix multiplies with a nonlinearity in between, stacked

    Watch out: Despite the name, it works very differently from a real brain.

    Two layers
    h = relu(W1 @ x + b1) y = W2 @ h + b2

  • Dataset splitstrain · validation · test

    The examples a model learns from get split three ways: a training set to learn from, a validation set to tune your settings, and a test set you only touch at the very end to see how the model really does.

    Everyday example: Practice questions, mock exams, and the real final. You don't get to see the final before the exam.

    Dev: Fixtures you never let leak into training

    Watch out: If test data leaks into training, your score is a lie.

    A common split
    train 80% · val 10% · test 10%

  • Loss functiona score for how wrong

    A single number that measures how far the model's predictions are from the right answers. The whole goal of training is to make that number smaller.

    Everyday example: The distance between your dart and the bullseye. Training is learning to shrink it.

    Dev: The metric your optimiser minimises

    Watch out: The model optimises exactly what the loss measures, which isn't always what you actually care about.

    Mean squared error
    L = 1n Σ (y − ŷ)2

  • Gradient descentwalking downhill

    The method for reducing the loss: work out which direction makes the error drop fastest (the gradient), take a small step that way, and repeat, thousands of times.

    Everyday example: Finding the bottom of a valley in thick fog by always stepping in whichever direction slopes down.

    Dev: Iteratively updating parameters against the gradient

    Watch out: It can settle in a dip that isn't the lowest point.

    One step
    θ ← θ − η ∇L

  • Learning ratethe step size

    How big each gradient descent step is. Too big and training overshoots and bounces around; too small and it takes forever to get anywhere.

    Everyday example: Big strides down a hill can jump straight past the bottom. Tiny shuffles get there, eventually.

    Dev: The η hyperparameter

    Watch out: A badly chosen learning rate is one of the most common reasons training fails.

    Default in PyTorch's Adam
    optim.Adam(params, lr=0.001)

  • Backpropagationassigning the blame

    The algorithm that works out how much each parameter contributed to the error, by passing the error backwards through the network, layer by layer. Gradient descent then uses those numbers to update every parameter.

    Everyday example: After a team loses, replaying the game to see which plays caused it, so every player knows exactly what to fix.

    Dev: Automatic differentiation via the chain rule

    Watch out: You almost never write it yourself. In PyTorch it's one line: loss.backward().

    In PyTorch
    loss.backward() # backprop optimizer.step() # update θ

  • Epochone full pass

    One complete pass through the whole training dataset. Models usually train for several epochs, seeing every example multiple times.

    Everyday example: Reading your whole textbook once, cover to cover. Revising for an exam usually takes a few reads.

    Dev: One iteration of the outer training loop

    Watch out: Too many epochs is a classic route to overfitting.

    In code
    for epoch in range(10): for batch in data: train_step(batch)

  • Hyperparameterssettings you choose

    The settings you pick before training starts (learning rate, number of layers, epochs, batch size), as opposed to parameters, which the model learns on its own.

    Everyday example: Oven temperature and baking time are yours to set. How the cake rises is up to the cake.

    Dev: Config you tune on the validation set

    Watch out: Tune them on the validation set, never on the test set.

    Example
    lr=1e-3, epochs=10, batch_size=32, layers=4

How LLMs work · 8 terms

Inside ChatGPT

The vocabulary of chatbots

Everything that happens between you hitting send and the reply appearing.

  • LLMlarge language model

    A huge neural network trained on enormous amounts of text to do one job: predict the next token. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are LLMs with a chat interface on top.

    Everyday example: Your phone keyboard suggesting the next word, scaled up enormously.

    Dev: A function from text to odds for what comes next

    Watch out: It predicts what's likely, not what's true.

    The job
    text → next token

  • Tokenhow models read text

    LLMs don't read words, they read tokens. Common words are a single token, rarer words get split into pieces, and every token is turned into a number.

    Everyday example: Reading a word you don't know by sounding out its pieces.

    Dev: Like an enum: every chunk of text has an ID

    Watch out: Pricing and limits are counted in tokens, not words. In English, a token is roughly three-quarters of a word.

    Example (illustrative)
    "unbelievable!" → [un][believ][able][!]

  • Context windowthe model's working memory

    The maximum amount of text, measured in tokens, a model can look at in one go: your prompt, the conversation so far, any documents you paste in, and its own reply.

    Everyday example: A desk that's only so big. Once it's full, the oldest papers start falling off the edge.

    Dev: The max input length the function accepts

    Watch out: Models can be worse at using details buried in the middle of very long inputs.

    The limit
    prompt + history + reply ≤ N tokens

  • Promptwhat you send the model

    Everything the model receives: your question plus any instructions, examples or documents. A system prompt is the hidden set of instructions an app adds before your message.

    Everyday example: Briefing a new colleague. "Write an email" gets you something generic; who it's for, why and how long gets you what you need.

    Dev: The function's arguments

    Watch out: The model can't read your mind. Missing context is the number one cause of bad answers.

    What the model sees
    [system] You are a helpful tutor. User: explain overfitting simply

  • Hallucinationconfident nonsense

    When a model states something false as if it were fact, like a citation that doesn't exist or a made-up number. It happens because the model generates likely-sounding text, not verified facts.

    Everyday example: A student bluffing an exam answer with total confidence.

    Dev: Plausible output with no grounding

    Watch out: Always check names, numbers and sources. RAG reduces hallucinations but doesn't remove them.

    Why it happens
    likely ≠ true

  • Temperaturethe randomness dial

    A setting that controls how random the model's word choices are. Low temperature gives safe, predictable answers; high temperature gives more varied, creative and sometimes stranger ones.

    Everyday example: Ordering your usual at a restaurant (low) versus trying something new on the menu (high).

    Dev: A divisor on the scores before sampling

    Watch out: Higher temperature doesn't make a model smarter, only more varied.

    With temperature T
    pi = ezi/TΣj ezj/T

  • Transformerthe design behind LLMs

    The neural network design behind almost every modern LLM: the T in GPT. Introduced in 2017 in the paper "Attention Is All You Need", its key idea is attention.

    Everyday example: Reading a whole paragraph at once, instead of one word at a time through a straw.

    Dev: Stacked blocks of attention + feed-forward layers

    Watch out: It's an architecture, not a product. GPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama are all built on it.

    Introduced
    2017 · "Attention Is All You Need"

  • Attentionwhat to focus on

    The mechanism that lets a model weigh which other words matter for the one it's processing. In "the animal didn't cross the street because it was tired", attention helps link "it" to "animal".

    Everyday example: At a noisy party, tuning in to the one voice that matters right now.

    Dev: A weighted average where the weights come from the data

    Watch out: Its cost grows with the square of the input length, one reason long inputs are expensive.

    Scaled dot-product attention
    softmax(QKT√d)V

Building with AI · 10 terms

What engineers build

The words you'll hear in AI engineering jobs

The techniques behind real AI products, from search over your documents to agents that take actions.

  • RAGretrieval-augmented generation

    Before answering, the system searches your documents for relevant passages and pastes them into the prompt, so the model answers from real sources instead of from memory.

    Everyday example: An open-book exam instead of a closed-book one.

    Dev: Search + prompt templating before the LLM call

    Watch out: If retrieval finds the wrong passages, the answer will be wrong, and confidently so.

    The idea
    docs = search(question) answer = llm(question + docs)

  • Vector databasesearch by meaning

    A database that stores embeddings and quickly finds the ones closest to a query, so you can search by meaning instead of exact keywords. It's the usual engine behind RAG.

    Everyday example: Asking a librarian for "books like this one" instead of searching by exact title.

    Dev: Nearest-neighbour search over vectors

    Watch out: For small projects you often don't need one: a plain list and NumPy is enough.

    A query
    index.query(embed("cheap flights"), top_k=5)

  • Fine-tuningspecialising a model

    Taking a model that's already trained and training it a bit more on your own smaller dataset, so it gets better at your task, tone or domain.

    Everyday example: A general doctor doing a specialisation: same training underneath, new expertise on top.

    Dev: Continued training from pretrained weights

    Watch out: Try good prompting and RAG first. Fine-tuning is slower and costlier to get right.

    The idea
    model = load("base-model") train(model, my_examples)

  • LoRAcheap fine-tuning

    Low-Rank Adaptation: instead of changing all of a model's billions of parameters, you freeze them and train a small set of extra ones on top. Far cheaper, and often doable on a single GPU.

    Everyday example: Clip-on lenses for your glasses instead of a whole new pair.

    Dev: Train a small low-rank add-on, freeze the rest

    Watch out: It's usually better for style and format than for teaching a model lots of new facts.

    The update
    W′ = W + BA  (B, A small)

  • AgentAI that takes actions

    An LLM running in a loop that can use tools (search the web, run code, call APIs), check the results, and decide what to do next until the task is done, instead of replying once.

    Everyday example: Asking someone to actually book your trip, versus asking them for travel tips.

    Dev: A while loop: think → call a tool → read the result

    Watch out: More autonomy means more ways to go wrong. Log every step.

    The loop
    while not done: action = llm(state) state += run_tool(action)

  • MCPModel Context Protocol

    An open standard, introduced by Anthropic in 2024, for connecting AI apps to tools and data. Build a tool once as an MCP server, and any app that supports MCP can use it.

    Everyday example: USB-C for AI: one plug that works across many devices.

    Dev: A client-server protocol (JSON-RPC) for tools and data

    Watch out: It only reaches tools that have an MCP server.

    The shape
    AI app ⇄ MCP ⇄ tools

  • RLHFlearning from human feedback

    Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people compare the model's answers, and the model is tuned toward the kind of answers people prefer. It's a big part of why chatbots feel helpful rather than like raw text predictors.

    Everyday example: A trainee getting thumbs up or down from a manager until they learn what "good" looks like.

    Dev: Train a reward model on preferences, then optimise against it

    Watch out: It can make models overly agreeable, telling people what they want to hear.

    The signal
    answer A ≻ answer B

  • Quantizationshrinking a model

    Storing a model's numbers with less precision (say 4 bits instead of 16) so it uses far less memory and runs faster, often well enough for a laptop, with a small hit to quality.

    Everyday example: Compressing a photo: a much smaller file that looks almost the same.

    Dev: float16 → int4

    Watch out: Push it too far and quality drops noticeably.

    Memory
    16-bit → 4-bit = ¼ the memory

  • Evalstesting AI

    Sets of test questions with known good answers, or scoring rules, used to measure how well a model or AI app performs and to catch it getting worse after a change.

    Everyday example: A driving test you rerun every time you change something about the car.

    Dev: A test suite for non-deterministic outputs

    Watch out: A high score on a public benchmark doesn't guarantee it will do well on your task.

    In code
    score = mean(grade(model(q), a) for q, a in tests)

  • Multimodalmore than text

    A model that can take in or produce more than one kind of data: text, images, audio or video. Uploading a photo and asking a question about it uses a multimodal model.

    Everyday example: Someone who can read, look and listen, not just read.

    Dev: Several input encoders feeding one model

    Watch out: It can still hallucinate, for example by confidently misreading a chart.

    The idea
    text + image → text
