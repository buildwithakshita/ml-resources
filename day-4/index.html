02
How I built it · plain English

Six steps, no magic

The same steps as almost every ML project

  1. Get the dataEvery F1 race result since 2021 from F1DB, a free database on GitHub.
  2. Clean itCrashes, retirements and disqualifications don't count toward form. A DNF says nothing about pace (sorry, Ferrari 2023).
  3. Build 4 featuresRecent finishes · recent points · car strength (team points per race) · track history at this Grand Prix.
  4. Don't trust one raceIf a driver has only raced here once, like Kimi in Austin, their track history is blended with current form.
  5. Train a random forestHundreds of decision trees vote on one question: podium, yes or no?
  6. Test it honestlyPredict every 2026 race using only data from before that race. Then predict Austin.

04
Learn it yourself

Random forests, explained

From "what's a decision tree" to training one

A random forest is a crowd of decision trees, each trained on a slightly different slice of the data. They vote, and the crowd is usually smarter than any one tree.

  • ~2 hours total
  • Free
Decision forests course Google Random forests StatQuest RandomForestClassifier scikit-learn
  1. Decision treesA flowchart of yes/no questions the model learns from data
  2. BaggingTrain many trees on random samples, then let them vote
  3. Feature importanceSee which inputs the forest relied on most (for me: recent points and car strength)
  4. Watch outWith rare outcomes like podiums, plain accuracy lies. Saying “no” to everyone is 86% accurate
